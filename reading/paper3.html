Working from home: the new normal?

The COVID-19 pandemic forced millions of workers around the world to move their offices into their homes almost overnight. What began as a temporary emergency measure has, for many, become a permanent feature of working life. As the immediate crisis faded, employers and employees found themselves engaged in a complex debate about what the future of work should look like.

Supporters of remote working argue that it offers employees greater flexibility and a better work-life balance. Without the daily commute, workers gain back significant amounts of time, often hours each week, which they can devote to family, leisure or additional rest. Research published in several countries suggests that many remote workers report higher levels of job satisfaction than their office-based counterparts.

However, not everyone has found working from home to be a positive experience. Those with small homes, demanding family responsibilities or limited access to reliable internet found remote work far more stressful than productive. Younger employees, in particular, often reported feeling isolated and missed the informal learning that comes from sharing a workspace with more experienced colleagues.

Employers, too, are divided. Some companies have seen no reduction in productivity and have embraced flexible arrangements enthusiastically. Others worry about maintaining company culture, managing performance and ensuring that junior staff receive the mentoring they need to develop professionally.

What seems clear is that a simple return to the pre-pandemic model is neither desirable nor realistic for most organisations. The challenge now is to develop hybrid models that capture the best elements of both office and home working, while protecting the wellbeing of all employees, regardless of their personal circumstances.

The Great Wall of China

The Great Wall of China is one of the most remarkable construction projects in human history. Stretching over 21,000 kilometres in total, it was not built as a single continuous structure but rather as a series of walls constructed by different Chinese states over many centuries.

The earliest sections were built as long ago as the seventh century BCE, when individual kingdoms erected barriers to defend their territories from northern nomadic tribes. It was during the reign of the first Emperor of China, Qin Shi Huang, in the third century BCE, that these separate walls were first connected into a more unified structure.

The most famous sections of the wall that exist today were largely constructed during the Ming Dynasty, which ruled from 1368 to 1644 CE. The Ming emperors rebuilt and extended the wall using fired bricks and stone, making it more durable than earlier earthen versions.

Contrary to popular belief, the Great Wall is not visible from space with the naked eye. This claim, which appears in many textbooks, has been confirmed as a myth by astronauts. The wall is simply too narrow, despite its great length, to be seen from that distance.
