The slow food movement

In 1989, the Italian journalist Carlo Petrini founded an organisation that would eventually grow into a worldwide movement with hundreds of thousands of members. He called it Slow Food, and its original purpose was straightforward: to oppose the rapid spread of fast-food culture and to defend the pleasures of eating well.

The movement began in Rome, when Petrini organised a protest against the opening of a McDonald's restaurant near the historic Spanish Steps. He and his supporters argued that the arrival of fast food in such a culturally significant location represented a threat not just to Italian cuisine but to the very idea of taking pleasure in food and its preparation.

Over the following decades, Slow Food expanded its mission considerably. What began as a defence of traditional cooking became an international campaign for sustainable agriculture, local food systems and biodiversity. The organisation now works with farmers, producers and communities in more than 150 countries.

Central to the movement's philosophy is the idea that food should be "good, clean and fair". By "good", Petrini means food that tastes good and is good for us. "Clean" refers to food produced sustainably, without damage to the environment. "Fair" means that everyone involved in producing food — from the farmer to the consumer — should receive a fair share of the benefits.

Critics argue that the movement is essentially elitist, appealing mainly to middle-class consumers who can afford to pay more for artisan products. Supporters counter that the true cost of cheap food, including environmental damage and exploitation of workers, is simply being paid elsewhere.

Sign language

Sign languages are complete, natural languages with their own grammar, vocabulary and expressive range. They are not simply versions of spoken languages expressed through hand gestures, but entirely independent communication systems that have evolved naturally within Deaf communities over many generations.

The most widely used sign language in the world is American Sign Language (ASL), which has around 500,000 users in North America. Despite its name, ASL is very different from British Sign Language (BSL), and users of one cannot typically understand users of the other. The two languages developed independently and share very little vocabulary.

Research into sign languages has contributed significantly to our understanding of human language in general. Studies of children who are born deaf show that they acquire sign language through the same developmental stages as hearing children acquire spoken language, suggesting that the capacity for language is innate rather than dependent on the ability to hear.

Sign languages also reveal much about the nature of grammar. Although they are expressed through movement and space rather than sound, they contain all the grammatical features found in spoken languages, including tense, aspect, negation and complex sentence structure.
