The science of sleep

Sleep is one of the most fundamental human needs, yet it remains among the least understood aspects of our biology. Scientists have been studying sleep for decades, and while much has been learned, the precise functions it serves continue to be the subject of active research and debate.

What is clear is that sleep is far from a passive state. During the night, the brain moves through a series of distinct stages, alternating between lighter and deeper phases of sleep and a period known as rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. It is during REM sleep that most vivid dreaming occurs, and researchers believe this phase plays a crucial role in processing emotions and consolidating memories.

The amount of sleep a person needs varies with age. Newborn babies may sleep for up to eighteen hours a day, while teenagers typically need around nine hours and most adults function best with seven to eight. This variation is controlled by biological processes rather than personal preference, which is why encouraging teenagers to attend early-morning classes may not be the most productive approach.

Chronic sleep deprivation — regularly getting less sleep than the body needs — has been linked to a wide range of health problems, including obesity, diabetes, heart disease and depression. The effects on cognitive function are also significant: sleep-deprived individuals perform poorly on tests of memory, attention and decision-making, sometimes to a degree that matches the effects of alcohol intoxication.

Despite the clear importance of sleep, modern life seems designed to disrupt it. Artificial light, particularly from screens, suppresses the hormone melatonin, which signals to the body that it is time to sleep. Shift work, long commutes and the pressure of work and social commitments all cut into the hours available for rest.

The history of coffee

Coffee is one of the most widely consumed beverages in the world, with over two billion cups drunk every day. Its origins can be traced to the highlands of Ethiopia, where, according to tradition, a goat herder named Kaldi first noticed the stimulating effects of the coffee plant when his flock became unusually energetic after eating its berries.

From Ethiopia, coffee spread to the Arabian Peninsula, where it was first cultivated on a large scale in the fifteenth century. By the sixteenth century, coffeehouses had become popular gathering places in cities across the Middle East, where people would meet to discuss business, politics and news. These establishments were sometimes called "Schools of the Wise".

Coffee reached Europe in the seventeenth century and quickly became enormously popular. London alone had over three hundred coffeehouses by 1675. These became important social institutions — places where ideas were exchanged, deals were made and newspapers were read.

The spread of coffee cultivation eventually extended to the Americas, where Brazil became, and remains, the world's largest producer. Today, coffee is grown in more than seventy countries across a tropical band known as the "coffee belt".
