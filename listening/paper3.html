Radio programme transcript
Presenter: "This week on Global Volunteers we're talking to Sophie Harris, who spent six months working at a wildlife conservation centre in Kenya. Sophie, what made you decide to go?"

Sophie: "I'd just finished my biology degree and I wanted real field experience before applying for jobs. The programme seemed like the perfect bridge between university and a career."

Presenter: "What were your main responsibilities?"

Sophie: "I monitored animal populations, helped with veterinary checks, and spent a lot of time collecting data on elephant movements in the reserve. I also taught environmental education sessions at local schools."

Presenter: "Were there any challenges?"

Sophie: "The heat was intense — we started work at five in the morning to avoid the worst of it. And I struggled with the language at first, though I picked up enough Swahili to manage by month two."

Presenter: "What did you gain from the experience?"

Sophie: "Confidence, mostly. And a much deeper understanding of conservation challenges that you simply cannot get from textbooks. I came back knowing exactly what kind of career I wanted."

Presenter: "Any advice for anyone considering it?"

Sophie: "Research your programme carefully — there are organisations that do it well and some that don't. And go with an open mind. The experience will almost certainly surprise you."

Part 4 Multiple Choice — Memory and Learning 7 marks

Read the transcript of a university lecture extract, then choose the correct answer for each question.

🎧 In the real exam, you hear this passage. Here you can read the transcript to practise.
Lecture transcript

Memory is not a single unified system but a collection of distinct processes. Psychologists distinguish between short-term memory, which can hold a small amount of information for a brief period, and long-term memory, which can store vast quantities of information for a lifetime. However, simply encountering information does not guarantee it will transfer from one system to the other. Most of what we experience each day is forgotten within hours.

The most reliable technique for moving information into long-term memory is called spaced repetition — reviewing material at increasing intervals over time. Research consistently shows that students who study a topic once for three hours perform significantly worse on tests a week later than students who study the same material for one hour on three separate occasions. The spacing effect, as it is known, is one of the most replicated findings in all of cognitive psychology.

A second powerful technique is retrieval practice — testing yourself on material rather than simply re-reading it. When you attempt to recall information, you strengthen the memory trace in a way that passive review cannot match. Students who use flashcards or take practice tests consistently outperform those who highlight and re-read their notes, despite spending the same amount of time studying.

Sleep also plays a critical role in memory consolidation. During sleep, the brain replays and strengthens the neural connections formed during learning. Studies have shown that students who sleep for eight hours after studying retain significantly more information the following day than those who sleep for less than six hours. Caffeine can help maintain alertness but does not replicate the memory benefits of adequate rest.

Finally, the physical environment in which you study matters more than most people realise. Studying in the same location repeatedly creates contextual cues that assist retrieval. However, research suggests that varying your study location can actually broaden the retrieval pathways associated with a memory, making it easier to recall in unfamiliar settings such as an exam room.

1. What does the speaker say about most daily experiences?

2. What is the key principle behind spaced repetition?

3. In the study comparing different approaches, which students performed better?

4. How does retrieval practice differ from passive review?

5. What does the research say about sleep and memory?

6. What is the benefit of always studying in the same place?

7. What surprising finding does the speaker mention about varying study locations?
