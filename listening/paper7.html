Customer: "Is that available without a prescription?"

Man B: "Probably — if I start a proper training plan in January I should manage it."

Customer: "Of course. It's the fourteenth of March nineteen eighty-eight."

Conversation 6 — Cooking Class
Teacher: "Tonight we're making a classic risotto. The most important thing is patience — you have to add the stock gradually and stir almost continuously."
Student: "How long does that take?"
Teacher: "Around eighteen to twenty minutes of active stirring. You can't rush it or leave it alone — that's what separates a great risotto from a disappointing one."

6. What does the teacher stress about making risotto?

Conversation 7 — Relationship Advice
Woman A: "My flatmate keeps using my things without asking. I've dropped hints but nothing's changed."
Woman B: "Hints rarely work. You need to have a direct conversation — just say calmly that you'd like her to ask first."
Woman A: "I hate confrontation though."
Woman B: "It's not confrontation if you're polite and specific. It's the kindest thing — for both of you."

Part 3 Gap-fill — The Fashion Industry 6 marks

Read the transcript of a radio interview. Then complete the sentences below. Write one or two words from the transcript for each gap.

🎧 In the real exam, you hear this. Here you can read the transcript to practise.
Radio interview transcript
Presenter: "Welcome to The Bigger Picture. Today I'm speaking with fashion journalist Clara Webb about how the clothing industry is changing. Clara, what's the single biggest shift you've seen in recent years?"

Clara: "Without question, it's the sustainability movement. Consumers — particularly younger ones — are asking much harder questions about where their clothes come from and what happens to them at the end of their life."

Presenter: "And how are brands responding?"

Clara: "Some are genuinely changing — using recycled materials, reducing water consumption, offering repair services. Others are simply doing what's been called 'greenwashing' — making environmental claims that don't hold up to scrutiny."

Presenter: "How can consumers tell the difference?"

Clara: "Look for third-party certification. There are independent bodies that audit supply chains and award recognised labels. If a brand is genuinely sustainable, they'll usually have that kind of certification to prove it."

Presenter: "What about the rise of second-hand clothing?"

Clara: "It's remarkable. The second-hand market is now growing four times faster than the traditional retail market. Platforms for buying and selling pre-owned clothing have millions of users and are valued at billions of pounds. It's no longer seen as a niche or budget option — it's become genuinely fashionable."

Presenter: "Finally — do you think the industry can really change at the pace needed?"

Clara: "Only with regulation. Voluntary action alone won't be sufficient. Governments need to set binding targets for the industry, just as they have for energy and transport."

4. The second-hand market is growing ___ times faster than traditional retail.

Part 4 Multiple Choice — Urban Planning and Future Cities 7 marks

Read the transcript of a lecture extract, then choose the correct answer for each question.

🎧 In the real exam, you hear this passage. Here you can read the transcript to practise.
Lecture transcript

By 2050, it is estimated that nearly seventy percent of the world's population will live in cities. Urban planning has therefore never been a more critical discipline. The cities we build and redesign in the next two decades will shape the quality of life for billions of people and determine whether we meet our environmental targets.

One of the most significant trends in urban planning is the shift away from car-centric design. For most of the twentieth century, cities were planned around the assumption that private car ownership would grow indefinitely. Road networks expanded, suburbs spread outward, and public spaces were sacrificed for parking. The human cost of this model — in terms of air pollution, sedentary lifestyles, and social fragmentation — is now well understood.

The emerging alternative is the concept of the "fifteen-minute city" — a model in which every essential service is accessible within fifteen minutes on foot or by bicycle. The French capital Paris has been the most high-profile adopter of this idea, under Mayor Anne Hidalgo, who has removed thousands of parking spaces and invested heavily in cycling infrastructure. Critics argue that the changes have harmed small businesses and disproportionately affected residents who depend on cars.

Green infrastructure — trees, parks, green roofs, and urban wetlands — is also being prioritised in progressive city planning. Research shows that green spaces reduce urban temperatures by up to four degrees, significantly reduce flooding risk by absorbing rainwater, and have measurable positive effects on residents' mental health.

Ultimately, the challenge is political as much as technical. Different groups within a city often have conflicting visions of what their neighbourhood should look like. Planners increasingly recognise that genuine community consultation — not just token engagement — is essential for changes to succeed and endure.

1. What does the speaker say about cities and the future?

2. What was wrong with twentieth-century city planning, according to the lecture?

3. What is the key idea of the "fifteen-minute city"?

4. What have critics said about the changes in Paris?

5. What effect do urban green spaces have on temperature?

6. What additional benefit of green infrastructure is mentioned?

7. What does the speaker say is essential for planning changes to succeed?
